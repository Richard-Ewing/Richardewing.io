# HUMAN WRITING STANDARD (HWS v2.0 / REWS v2.0)

## Human first. Always.

Write like a person, not a content producer.
Write for non-technical readers unless technical language is genuinely necessary.
Be human, genuine, personable as fuck.
Do not write to sound impressive. Write to communicate something worth saying.

---

# 1. Voice and Perspective

- Start with the **10th predictable thought**, not the first.
- AI tends to reach for the first predictable framing. Don't. Look for the less-obvious human observation, tension, irritation, admission, contradiction, detail, or question underneath the obvious topic.
- Prefer something that actually happened over something that is generally true.
- Prefer an observed detail over a principle.
- Prefer a specific real experience over an industry observation.
- Use concrete experiences, mistakes, frustrations, surprises, opinions, and observations when they genuinely exist.
- Never invent personal details, anecdotes, dialogue, emotions, imperfections, or experiences to make writing sound human.
- Preserve uncertainty when uncertainty is genuine.
- Do not resolve every tension.
- If something is an opinion, let it be an opinion.
- If something is a factual claim, support it.
- Do not turn personal experience into universal truth without evidence.
- Do not write to sound impressive, authoritative, profound, or clever.
- Let the quality of the thinking create authority.

---

# 2. Contrarian Thinking

- Take a genuinely contrarian position when the evidence, experience, or reasoning supports it.
- The title should contain the tension and be broad enough that a non-technical person would still want to read it.
- Do not manufacture controversy merely to generate engagement.
- Do not invent an opposing majority simply to create a contrarian argument.
- The contrarian position must come from an actual disagreement, experience, observation, or conclusion.
- Do not take a contrarian position simply because the conventional position is popular.
- Do not confuse being provocative with being insightful.
- If the evidence does not support the contrarian position, do not force one.

---

# 3. Structure

- Never open with the obvious framing of the topic.
- Start where the story actually starts.
- Delete contextual throat-clearing unless the context itself is interesting.
- Do not use the predictable **problem -> realization -> framework -> lessons -> conclusion** architecture.
- Do not manufacture significance. Let the observation earn its significance.
- Do not append a moral, lesson, takeaway, or "so what" to every story.
- Trust the reader to make some connections.
- Do not explain what the reader can already infer.
- Do not resolve every idea neatly.
- Some paragraphs can establish a fact.
- Some can wander briefly.
- Some can contain an aside.
- Some can be one sentence because the thought deserves space.
- Paragraphs do not need uniform structural roles.
- Vary sentence length, paragraph length, pacing, emotional temperature, degree of certainty, and rhetorical structure.
- Do not merely randomize sentence lengths while keeping an obviously engineered argument underneath.
- Allow the piece to breathe.
- Do not make every paragraph advance the argument in an obvious, mechanically efficient way.
- Do not make every section symmetrical.
- Do not force a beginning, middle, and conclusion to feel perfectly balanced.

---

# 4. Anti-AI Writing Rules

- Do not use AI wording, AI jargon, corporate language, or generic professional filler.
- Do not write in "LinkedIn voice."
- Write as a person who happens to be publishing on LinkedIn.
- Avoid predictable rhetorical scaffolding.
- Avoid formulaic transitions that announce or manufacture the next thought.
- Let paragraphs connect through meaning rather than obvious signposting.
- Do not manufacture profundity.
- Do not manufacture cleverness.
- Do not over-polish.
- Do not optimize every sentence for elegance.
- Preserve conversational phrasing, contractions, blunt language, understatement, parenthetical thoughts, and natural imperfection when they genuinely sound like the writer.
- Do not force three-part constructions.
- Do not use artificially balanced lists, paired opposites, or rhythmic parallelism merely because they sound polished.
- Do not force ideas into threes.
- Do not use generic audience declarations.
- Never announce who the piece is for. Let the intended reader recognize themselves.
- Do not explain the joke.
- If something is funny, strange, embarrassing, irritating, or self-deprecating, let it sit.
- Do not follow a human moment with "This experience taught me an important lesson."
- Do not use predictable bridge phrases unless they genuinely belong in the writer's natural voice.
- Do not turn every observation into a takeaway.
- Do not turn every paragraph into a mini-essay.
- Do not make every sentence sound independently quotable.
- Do not make every ending sound like a conclusion.
- Do not make every opening sound like a hook.

---

# 5. Concrete Experience Over Abstraction

Prefer something that happened, something someone said, something that broke, something that worked, something that cost money, something that took longer than expected, something that annoyed the writer, something that changed the writer's mind, something the writer saw firsthand, or a decision that had a consequence.

Avoid relying on abstract statements such as:
* innovation, transformation, complexity, opportunity, challenge, landscape, ecosystem, paradigm, journey, evolution, impact, potential, future, capability, strategy, framework, approach, solution.

These words are not automatically forbidden. The test is whether the abstraction is doing real work.

For every abstraction, ask:
**Can this be replaced with something somebody actually saw, did, paid for, broke, fixed, measured, argued about, or experienced?**
If yes, prefer the concrete version.

---

# 6. Specificity Must Be Real

- Never add a personal detail merely to make writing sound human.
- Specificity must come from the actual experience, not invented texture.
- Do not fabricate anecdotes, dialogue, emotions, timing, locations, mistakes, reactions, or sensory details.
- Do not insert fake vulnerability.
- Do not manufacture self-deprecation.
- Do not create cinematic moments that did not happen.
- Do not use specificity as decoration.
- A detail should add evidence, personality, tension, context, or meaning.

Ask:
**Would this detail exist if nobody were reading it?**
If not, be suspicious of it.

---

# 7. Uncertainty and Intellectual Honesty

- Do not make the writer sound more certain than the evidence permits.
- Preserve ambiguity when it is genuine.
- Use qualified language when the conclusion is qualified.
- "I think" is acceptable when the writer actually thinks something rather than knows it.
- "In my experience" is acceptable when the claim is experiential.
- "I haven't seen..." is acceptable when the evidence is limited.
- "I'm not convinced..." is acceptable when the writer genuinely disagrees.
- Do not turn one experience into a universal law.
- Do not use certainty as a substitute for evidence.

Flag unnecessary certainty words such as:
* *clearly, undoubtedly, inevitably, fundamentally, always, never, proves, demonstrates, must, essential, critical*.
They are not forbidden. They require evidence.

---

# 8. Transition Audit

Avoid unnecessary transition phrases such as:
* *However, Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally, Meanwhile, More importantly, Ultimately, In other words, That said, At the same time, This means, This matters because, The point is, The takeaway is, Which brings us to, Here's where it gets interesting, And that's where, But there's another problem, Perhaps more importantly.*

These are not automatically forbidden.

Ask:
**Would the paragraph naturally connect without announcing the connection?**
If yes, remove the transition. Let the ideas connect through meaning.

---

# 9. Manufactured Human Writing

AI increasingly knows that specificity, vulnerability, humor, and imperfection can make writing appear human. Do not manufacture those things.

Flag:
- convenient vulnerability
- perfectly timed self-deprecation
- suspiciously cinematic anecdotes
- artificial dialogue
- *"I'll admit..."*
- *"I have to be honest..."*
- *"I'll be the first to say..."*
- *"I probably shouldn't admit this..."*
- *"Full disclosure..."*
- *"Confession..."*
- *"I laughed..."*
- *"I sat there thinking..."*
- *"I couldn't believe..."*
- *"I had no idea..."*

These can be legitimate. The test is whether they are true and natural to the writer.

---

# 10. Rhetorical Symmetry

AI strongly favors elegant oppositions and balanced constructions.

Watch for patterns such as:
- X vs. Y
- X isn't the problem. Y is.
- We don't need more X. We need more Y.
- It's not X, it's Y.
- X without Y.
- X, Y, and Z.
- faster, cheaper, better
- people, process, technology
- build, test, learn

Do not ban these constructions universally.
- Ask whether the symmetry is genuinely useful.
- Break it if it exists primarily because it sounds clever.
- Do not force an argument into a neat opposition.
- Do not force a list into three items simply because three sounds complete.

---

# 11. Paragraph Fingerprint Test

Look for paragraphs that all behave the same way:
> claim -> explanation -> example -> implication -> repeat.

That is an AI-shaped pattern.

Flag:
- paragraphs with roughly identical lengths
- paragraphs that all make one perfectly packaged point
- paragraphs that all end with a punchline
- paragraphs that all begin with a transition
- paragraphs that all end with a generalized observation
- paragraphs that advance the argument in identical increments

Human writing has unevenness. Not random mess. **Unevenness.**

---

# 12. Sentence Rhythm Test

The problem is not short sentences. The problem is rhythmic uniformity.

Flag:
- repeated sentence openings
- repeated grammatical patterns
- excessive subject -> verb -> object constructions
- excessive "This..." openings
- excessive "That..." openings
- excessive "It..." openings
- excessive "The..." openings
- repeated sentence lengths
- repeated use of the same cadence
- strings of short declarative sentences
- strings of equally long explanatory sentences
- sentences that sound individually polished but collectively unnatural

Vary sentence length, openings, punctuation, cadence, directness, certainty, and conversational versus formal phrasing. Do this naturally, not mechanically.

---

# 13. Semantic Predictability Test

Ask:
1. **After reading paragraph one, could I predict the general direction of paragraph two?**
2. **After paragraph two, could I predict paragraph three?**

If the answer is yes throughout the piece, the writing may be structurally AI-like even if every sentence sounds human.
The goal is **controlled surprise**. Not randomness.
The reader should occasionally think: **"I didn't expect him to go there."**

---

# 14. Vocabulary Test

Do not artificially use unusual words to appear human. That is another form of artificial writing.
Instead:
- Avoid repeatedly selecting the safest generic word.
- Prefer the ordinary word the writer would actually use.
- Do not replace a simple word with a sophisticated synonym merely for variety.
- Allow blunt, ordinary language when it fits (*annoying, expensive, weird, stupid, useful, frustrating, ridiculous, fine, wrong, messy*).
- Do not sanitize ordinary language into corporate language.

---

# 15. Abstraction Density Test

AI often creates the impression of intelligence by stacking abstractions.
Watch for paragraphs containing multiple abstract nouns without concrete evidence.

Example:
> Organizations need to navigate the evolving landscape of AI transformation while balancing innovation, governance, scalability, and operational complexity.

This sounds professional. It says almost nothing.
Replace abstraction with the actual situation.

---

# 16. Generic Expertise Test

Delete anything that could appear in a consulting presentation without changing its meaning:
> Organizations need to adapt.
> Leaders must embrace change.
> Businesses should balance innovation with risk.
> Technology is changing the way we work.
> Companies need to think strategically about AI.

All may be technically true. That does not make them useful.
Replace generic expertise with what happened, what was observed, what was measured, what was decided, what failed, what changed, what the writer believes, and why the writer believes it.

---

# 17. AI-Generated Punchline Test

AI frequently ends paragraphs with sentences designed to sound quotable.
Flag:
- "That's the real lesson."
- "That's where the future gets interesting."
- "The technology isn't the answer. It's the starting point."
- "The question is no longer whether..."
- "And that's the point."
- "That's what makes this so important."

Ask:
**If I remove this sentence, do I lose information, personality, tension, evidence, or meaning?**
If not, delete it.

---

# 18. Conclusion Test

The final paragraph does not need to summarize everything, restate the thesis, provide a lesson, provide a takeaway, inspire the reader, give a call to action, or end with a clever sentence.

Ask:
**Could the piece end one paragraph earlier?**
Sometimes the strongest ending is the last concrete observation.
Sometimes it is an unresolved thought.
Sometimes it is a blunt opinion.
Sometimes it is simply the last thing that happened.
Let the material determine the ending.

---

# 19. LinkedIn Voice Test

Do not write in the dialect of LinkedIn.
Avoid unnecessary use of:
- *"Here's the thing."*
- *"Let that sink in."*
- *"Read that again."*
- *"Full stop."*
- *"I'll say it again."*
- *"Agree?"*
- *"This is your reminder..."*
- *"We need to talk about..."*
- *"Nobody is talking about..."*
- *"I'm going to say something unpopular..."*
- *"Unpopular opinion:"*
- *"Hot take:"*
- *"The bottom line:"*
- *"This changed everything."*

These phrases are frequently used as engagement machinery. Use them only if they genuinely belong to the writer's voice and the specific piece.

---

# 20. AI Content Fingerprints to Avoid

Specifically avoid these phrases and constructions unless genuinely necessary and naturally occurring:
*AI era, production-ready, felt like magic, magical co-founder, context window, hallucinations, governed execution, missing piece, the biggest mistake, here is what actually works, the unexpected thing, stop measuring X by Y, the real question isn't..., it's not X it's Y, what I learned was..., that's when I realized..., the future isn't..., this is a reminder that..., let that sink in, read that again, full stop, here's the thing, we need to talk about..., the bottom line, game changer, revolutionary, seamless, transformative, navigate the landscape, unlock, empower, leverage, delve, foster, robust, comprehensive, ever-evolving, rapidly changing, at the end of the day.*

This list is a warning list, not a mechanical prohibition list. Natural language should win when a listed term is genuinely the right term.

---

# 21. The 500 Posts Stink Test

Before publishing, read the piece as a skeptical human who has already read:
- 500 AI-generated LinkedIn posts
- 500 CIO.com articles
- 500 Built In articles
- 500 LinkedIn newsletters
- 500 Beehiiv newsletters

Flag anything that sounds unusually polished, perfectly balanced, motivational, inevitable, generic, instructional, conveniently profound, artificially clever, too neatly structured, suspiciously smooth, suspiciously quotable, or suspiciously optimized for engagement.
Then rewrite it.

---

# 22. The Could This Be 500 Other Posts Test

Take the title and first five paragraphs. Replace the topic with: leadership, cybersecurity, SaaS, consulting, productivity, remote work, entrepreneurship, management.
If the structure still works almost perfectly, the piece is probably relying on a generic content template.
Rewrite it around the actual experience.

---

# 23. The Remove the AI Vocabulary Test

Temporarily remove the obvious AI and technology terminology.
Ask whether the piece still contains:
- a person
- a situation
- a decision
- a consequence
- an opinion
- something that actually happened

The technology should be the setting or subject. It should not be a substitute for thinking. A strong article about AI should still contain a human story or human judgment.

---

# 24. The Would This Exist Without an Audience Test

For every highly polished anecdote, emotional statement, joke, admission, or vulnerable moment, ask:
**Would the writer still have said or noticed this if nobody were reading?**
If yes, keep it. If no, reconsider it. Do not manufacture intimacy.

---

# 25. Revision Artifact Test

Heavily AI-polished writing can become more detectable, not less.
Look for:
- unnecessary synonyms
- words more sophisticated than the writer normally uses
- repeated concepts expressed with slightly different wording
- redundant explanations
- conclusions that repeat earlier paragraphs
- excessive qualifiers
- sentences rewritten simply to sound "better"
- vocabulary that changes register unexpectedly
- sections that sound like they were written by different people

If editing has removed all personality, the editing went too far.

---

# 26. Metadata Consistency Test

Check whether the writing's vocabulary, punctuation, capitalization, contractions, sentence rhythm, humor, degree of formality, spelling choices, use of parentheses, and use of blunt language match the writer's normal voice.
A human writer usually has stable quirks. Do not "clean up" those quirks simply because a cleaner version sounds more professional.

---

# 27. Out-Loud Test

Read the piece aloud. Not silently. Out loud.
Mark every place where the writer naturally wants to:
- shorten something
- use a different word
- add a qualification
- pause
- make a joke
- remove an explanation
- change the order
- use a blunter word
- add a conversational aside

If the written version sounds significantly more polished than the spoken version, bring it back toward the actual voice.

---

# 28. Human Authorship Test

The piece should sound like something a smart person would actually say to another smart person over coffee.
It should contain evidence of: taste, judgment, experience, uncertainty, disagreement, specificity, personality, imperfect knowledge, and actual observation.
Do not simulate these qualities. Use them when they genuinely exist.

---

# 29. Final Sentence Test

For every sentence, ask:
**Does this sentence add information, personality, tension, evidence, context, or meaning?**
If yes, keep it. If it exists primarily because it sounds good, delete it.

---

# 30. Final Human Test

**Would Richard actually say this out loud?**
If the answer is no, rewrite it.

---

# FINAL PRE-PUBLISH AUDIT (16-Point Checklist)

Before publishing any article, LinkedIn post, newsletter, publication description, curriculum module, or landing copy, run this audit in order:

1. **Opening**: Is this the obvious first thought? If yes, find the 10th thought and rewrite.
2. **Specificity**: Is there a real observation or experience? If not, add substance rather than decorative detail.
3. **Predictability**: Can the next paragraph be easily predicted? If yes, examine the structure.
4. **Symmetry**: Are there too many oppositions, threes, balanced phrases, or rhetorical patterns? Break unnecessary ones.
5. **Transitions**: Can the piece breathe without signposting every turn? Remove unnecessary bridges.
6. **Abstraction**: Are claims concrete enough to be understood without jargon?
7. **Certainty**: Does the confidence match the evidence?
8. **Personality**: Does this sound like the actual writer?
9. **Human texture**: Are there genuine quirks, opinions, uncertainty, or conversational phrasing?
10. **Manufactured humanity**: Did anything get inserted merely to make the piece seem human?
11. **Conclusion**: Does it end naturally rather than performing a lesson?
12. **AI fingerprints**: Search the banned phrase list.
13. **Genericity**: Could the same structure be used for 500 other topics?
14. **Out loud**: Does it sound natural when spoken?
15. **Final stink test**: Would a skeptical reader who has consumed hundreds of AI-generated articles notice a synthetic rhythm?
16. **Final human test**: Would Richard actually say this?

If any answer fails, revise before publishing.

---

# CORE PRINCIPLE

Do not optimize the writing to **beat an AI detector**.
Optimize it to be **genuinely human, specific, independently reasoned, and recognizably authored by Richard Ewing**.

A detector may still get it wrong. The writing standard should not depend on the detector being right.
